PROJECT 1 FILE MCMASTER MARS ROVER

Rover Electrical Systems

ELEC • POWER • PCB DESIGN
Altium Designer PSpice STM32 MOSFET Protection NVIDIA Jetson AGX SPARK MAX Ethernet

About the Project

The McMaster Mars Rover Team builds a Mars rover for competition. My job is to design, build, and wire the rover's electrical system: everything that gets power to the rover's parts and lets them work together.

Why It Matters

A rover can only do its job if its electronics work every time. Every motor, camera, and computer on board needs the right amount of power, delivered safely, and a reliable way to send information. If the electrical system fails, the rover stops, no matter how good the rest of it is.

The Battery System

The rover runs on a battery, so I designed and assembled a battery distribution system: the part that takes power from the battery and sends it to the rest of the rover. It is built for 48 volts and 40 amps (about 1.9 kilowatts of power) and supplies 8 different rover subsystems.

It also includes a protection switch, called a high-side MOSFET, between the battery and the rest of the rover. This lets power be controlled and keeps the electronics protected.

The Custom Circuit Boards

I designed 2 custom circuit boards (PCBs) in Altium Designer. That meant drawing the circuit diagram, laying out the connections on the board, and testing the circuit in a simulator called PSpice before building anything, so mistakes are caught on the computer instead of on the real hardware.

One of the boards is the rover's Batman board (for the battery). It is controlled by a small programmable chip (an STM32 microcontroller).

1 / 4

Customs PCB's Developed for the Rover.

Computer, Motors, and Wiring

A rover needs a brain and muscles. I integrated the NVIDIA Jetson AGX, the rover's onboard computer, with the SPARK MAX motor controllers, which control how the DC motors run, and with devices that connect over Ethernet, a wired network connection.

Across all of this I completed more than 300 soldered connections, joining wires and components by hand so the whole system works together.

1 / 3

Components integrated together to make sure the rover operates efficiently.

How It All Connects

Put simply, power flows from the battery, through protection and power distribution hardware, and out to the rover's 8 subsystems. At the same time, the Jetson computer, the motor controllers, and the Ethernet-connected devices are wired in so they can work together.

Making It All Fit

An electrical system has to work inside a real rover, not just on a desk. That means routing cables neatly, keeping parts easy to reach for repairs, and fitting everything into the limited space of the chassis.

I work with the electrical, software, and other teams so every part connects properly and can be built, tested, and used on the rover.

At a Glance

  • 01 48 V, 40 A battery distribution system with high-side MOSFET protection
  • 02 8 rover subsystems supplied with power
  • 03 2 custom PCBs designed in Altium Designer, including an STM32-controlled power distribution board
  • 04 300+ soldered connections across the rover's electrical system

Where It Stands

This is ongoing work. I continue to design, build, and wire the rover's electrical system with the team.

PROJECT 00 FILE HACK THE NORTH 2026 — TOP 32 OF 350 TEAMS

VendiGo — Autonomous Mobile Storefront

ROBOTICS • VOICE AI • FULL-STACK
ESP32 STM32 Raspberry Pi Zero Voice AI Next.js Python Autonomous Navigation Motor Control

Inspiration

At crowded events like stadiums, festivals, and hackathons, buying food often means leaving your seat, finding a booth, and waiting in line. Human vendors can bring products directly to customers, but they're expensive to operate and difficult to scale. We wanted to build a storefront that could move directly to where demand is.

What It Does

VendiGo is an autonomous mobile storefront for crowded events. It drives toward customers, uses a voice agent to advertise products and answer questions using live inventory data, and lets customers purchase items through a QR-based storefront. When a purchase is made, inventory updates automatically, and operators can monitor purchases, inventory levels, and customer activity through a live dashboard.

1 / 8

Selected Build Photos of VendiGo.

How We Built It

We built VendiGo on an ACEBOTT robotic car platform, using an STM32 running PWM motor control firmware alongside an ESP32, sensors, and cameras, with a Raspberry Pi Zero handling navigation, voice interaction, and system coordination. We added a custom microphone and speaker setup so VendiGo can continuously listen and talk with nearby customers, using ElevenLabs for speech and an LLM for conversation grounded in live inventory context. The robot also includes an automatically opening product compartment that unlocks during checkout and closes after pickup. On the software side, a Next.js web app powers the QR storefront, live inventory, purchase flow, analytics, and operator dashboard, tying the hardware and customer experience into one autonomous retail system.

Challenges We Ran Into

Our biggest challenge was coordinating autonomous navigation, voice interaction, and the checkout flow into one seamless experience. We also had to manage microphone and speaker timing, keep inventory synchronized after purchases, and connect live inventory data to the voice agent so it wouldn't recommend sold-out products.

Accomplishments We're Proud Of

We brought robotics, conversational AI, and commerce together into one working prototype. Customers can speak directly with VendiGo, ask what products are available, and purchase from the robot, while operators can monitor inventory and purchases through a live dashboard, achieving 90% navigation success across 50+ test runs and 100% inventory-update success across 80 test transactions.

What We Learned

We learned how to connect hardware, web software, and conversational AI into a single real-world system, and how important latency, timing, and clear state management become when software has to interact with people through a physical robot.

What's Next

We want to deploy VendiGo at real events and eventually bring the system to food and beverage companies, event venues, festivals, and sports stadiums. Next, we plan to improve autonomous navigation and speech recognition in noisy crowds, integrate live payments, support more products and languages, and use historical sales and location data to predict where demand will be highest, so VendiGo can move autonomously to where it's most likely to make a sale.
